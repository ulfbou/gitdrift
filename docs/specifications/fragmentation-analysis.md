# Fragmentation analysis

## Purpose

Fragmentation analysis accounts for how a coherent artifact or concept representation becomes distributed, transformed, duplicated, retained, or lost across later states.

## Source and descendants

An analysis identifies a source state, source artifacts or fragments, descendant states, candidate descendant representations, correspondence methods, and completeness.

## Accounting classes

### Transferred

A supported source fact or representation appears in a descendant with equivalent relevant meaning and role.

### Transformed

A descendant preserves relevant meaning through a changed structure or expression. Transformation requires semantic correspondence evidence.

### Duplicated

A source representation corresponds to more than one descendant. Duplication is not automatically harmful, but later contradiction and authority ambiguity are analyzed separately.

### Residual

Source material remains after other content moves. Residue may be active, historical, explanatory, obsolete, or unresolved according to evidence.

### Divergent

Descendants corresponding to the same source become semantically inconsistent, differently authoritative, stronger, weaker, or contradictory.

### Unaccounted

Relevant source content has no supported descendant within analyzed scope. Unaccounted does not equal lost when lineage or extraction completeness is partial.

### Authoritative and non-authoritative

Every representation role is retained. Similar text in authority, implementation, explanation, generated output, or historical evidence is not interchangeable.

## Split and merge

One-to-many and many-to-one relations preserve all source and target identities. The analysis records overlap, transformation, duplication, conflict, and unresolved attribution.

## Case construction

A possible-loss-during-fragmentation case includes source evidence, descendant inventory, accounting, obligations, authority, counterevidence, unknowns, and completeness. Healthy decomposition remains a valid outcome.
